= RFD 17 Attribute-based authorization

== Goal

Let an organization decide who may see and do what in which part of its
inventory.
Today every member holds one of four fixed roles (`owner`, `admin`, `member`,
`viewer`), and each context checks a hard-coded list of them. That cannot
express common arrangements: a network team that edits switches but not
power, facilities staff who place hardware at one site, or a contractor who may
only see and operate a few hosts for two weeks.

This RFD replaces the role lists with one model. A role is a named combination
of permissions. Attributes of the person decide which roles they hold, and
attributes of the resource decide where each role applies. RFDs 12, 13, and 14
already need scoped, expiring grants, and RFDs 15 and 16 grant access from an
identity provider; all of them become instances of this model instead of
separate grant tables.

Authorization stays where it is today: in context functions, inside the
transaction that performs the change. Hiding a control in the UI remains a hint,
never the boundary.

== Permissions

A permission names one kind of action, such as `inventory.edit` or
`topology.edit`. The catalogue is defined in code, not by organizations, and
every public context function that changes data checks exactly one permission
or is an explicit system path.

The first release derives the catalogue from today's checks:

[cols="2,4"]
|===
|Permission |Allows

|`inventory.read`
|Seeing resources and everything that belongs to them (see <<reads>>)

|`inventory.reconcile`
|Reconciling observations into inventory, and accepting reconciliation results
 for topology and catalog

|`inventory.edit`
|Changing lifecycle state, setting and clearing field overrides, and creating
 or changing managed resource kinds

|`findings.act`
|Assigning, snoozing, and accepting exceptions on findings

|`requests.create`
|Proposing a change the person cannot make directly

|`requests.decide`
|Approving or rejecting proposals

|`catalog.author`
|Catalog types and drafts, modules, inventory items, and moves

|`catalog.expectations`
|Expected-component exceptions and automatic moves

|`dcim.edit`
|Sites, locations, racks, and placement

|`ipam.edit`
|VRFs, prefixes, addresses, plans, and source mappings

|`topology.edit`
|VLANs, cables, cable plans, and cable import contracts

|`jobs.request`
|Requesting, cancelling, and resolving `observe` jobs

|`views.share`
|Saving and changing shared views

|`teams.manage`
|Teams, team membership, and resource owners

|`triage_rules.manage`
|Triage rules

|`intake_keys.manage`
|Intake API keys

|`members.manage`
|Inviting and disabling members and changing their assignments

|`roles.manage`
|Defining custom roles

|`organization.manage`
|Organization settings, including job settings and appearance
|===

Relationship checks such as "only the person who
made a request may withdraw it" and "a personal view belongs to its owner" stay
in context code; they are not permissions.

RFDs 12, 13, and 14 add `queries.run`, `queries.run_sensitive`,
`terminal.open`, `vm.power`, and `vm.approve` when they are implemented. Those
are _explicit_ permissions: no built-in role includes them, nobody holds them by
default, including owners, and an assignment that grants one must expire. Each
explicit permission declares its longest validity, such as RFD 13's 30 days.

== Roles

A role is a named set of permissions within an organization.

The four built-in roles keep today's behavior exactly and cannot be edited:

[cols="3,1,1,1,1"]
|===
|Permission |`owner` |`admin` |`member` |`viewer`

|`inventory.read` |yes |yes |yes |yes
|`inventory.reconcile`, `findings.act`, `catalog.author` |yes |yes |yes |
|`requests.create` | | |yes |
|`inventory.edit`, `requests.decide`, `catalog.expectations`, `dcim.edit`,
 `ipam.edit`, `topology.edit`, `jobs.request`, `views.share`, `teams.manage`,
 `triage_rules.manage`, `intake_keys.manage` |yes |yes | |
|`members.manage` |yes |yes | |
|`roles.manage`, `organization.manage` |yes | | |
|===

`members.manage` and `roles.manage` are new; Renga has no member management UI
today. An admin may manage members but may not grant `owner` or any permission
the admin does not hold (see <<administration>>).

Owners can define custom roles from any non-explicit permissions,
for example a `network engineer` role with `inventory.reconcile`,
`findings.act`, `ipam.edit`, and `topology.edit`. Explicit permissions live in
their own roles, so a long-lived role never quietly carries terminal or power
access.

== Assignments

An assignment gives one role to a subject, optionally limited by a condition
and an expiry:

* _role_: a built-in or custom role;
* _subject_: one member, a team, a directory group (RFD 16), or a mapped
  claim value (RFD 15);
* _condition_: which resources it covers (below); none means the whole
  organization;
* _valid until_: optional, required for explicit permissions;
* _source_: `manual`, a claim mapping, or a directory connection, with the
  same source-bound validity rules RFDs 15 and 16 define;
* who created it, when, and why.

Each membership keeps one base role, as today, which acts as an assignment
without a condition. A membership may instead have no base role, so that it
holds only what its other assignments grant (see <<reads>>). Every further assignment adds access; the result is the
union of everything a person holds. There are no deny rules, so the question
"why can this person do this" always has a list of assignments as its answer.
A manual assignment is never changed or removed by a claim mapping or directory
connection; an externally sourced one is removed when its source no longer
grants it.

Teams gain members. A team is already the owner of resources (RFD 8); adding
people to it lets one assignment say "people on a team may operate what their
team owns". Team membership is managed with `teams.manage`, and a directory
connection may manage the membership of teams it is mapped to.

== Conditions

A condition is a set of clauses over resource attributes. A resource matches
when it satisfies every clause, and a clause is satisfied by any of its values:

[source,json]
----
{
  "site": ["<site id>"],
  "kind": ["host", "switch"],
  "owner_team": ["$subject_teams"]
}
----

This reads as "hosts and switches at this site that are owned by one of the
person's teams", and the UI renders it that way.

The first release supports these attributes:

* `resource`: explicit resource IDs;
* `kind`: resource kinds;
* `owner_team`: team IDs, or `$subject_teams` for the teams the person belongs
  to;
* `site`: site IDs, matched through the resource's current placement, or the
  resource itself for sites, locations, and racks.

Conditions are deliberately a small structured language rather than a general
policy language such as Cedar or Rego. Lists, search, and bulk actions must find
what a person may act on in the database, so every condition compiles to an SQL
predicate over indexed columns. A richer language would either leak into
per-row checks in Elixir or need its own partial evaluator.

A permission is checked against the resource an action targets. An action whose
target is organization-wide, such as defining a catalog type, a VRF, a site, a
team, or a triage rule, requires the permission from an assignment without a
condition. Placement, cables, and other actions that involve several resources
must be covered for each of them.

Conditions never read agent-reported data such as resource labels. An
organization configures its agents but does not fully control them, and anyone
holding its intake key can report any label. Boundaries that cut across sites
and teams, such as environment, customer, or compliance scope, are left to
RFD 18's resource groups.

=== Trusted attributes

Some attributes can be derived from observations: triage rules set owner teams
from reported data, and placement may come from observed evidence. Inventory
workflow permissions may match those derived values. Operational permissions,
meaning `jobs.request` and the explicit permissions, only match values a person
set or confirmed: explicit resource IDs, manually set or approved owner teams,
confirmed placement. This preserves RFD 13's rule that
grants authorize against trusted bindings.

=== Changing attributes

Changing an attribute that a condition reads (owner team or placement) requires
the actor to cover the resource both before and after the change. Someone who may edit hosts at one site cannot move a host to another site
they do not cover, and cannot move one into their own scope. Approving a request
applies the same rule to the approver.

== Reads

`inventory.read` is a permission like any other, and conditions limit it. A
person sees a resource only when one of their assignments grants
`inventory.read` with a condition the resource matches. Every built-in role
includes it, so a member whose base role is built-in still reads the whole
organization, as today. A contractor can instead be a member with no base role
and a `viewer` assignment for hosts at one site, and sees nothing else.

What a resource's visibility covers:

* Everything that belongs to one resource follows it: its fields, observations
  and evidence, interfaces, components and modules, assigned addresses,
  placement, findings, requests, jobs, and Activity entries.
* Sites, locations, and racks are resources and follow the same rule, except
  that a person also sees the containers of every resource they can see, so its
  placement can be shown.
* Organization-wide definitions (catalog types, VRFs, prefixes, VLAN groups and
  VLANs, and teams) are shared vocabulary, visible to anyone who holds
  `inventory.read` anywhere.
* Records that join several resources, such as cables, neighbor adjacency, and
  power paths, show only the endpoints the person can see. A hidden endpoint
  appears as not visible to them, without its name or ID.
* Aggregates over a shared container, such as rack occupancy and prefix
  utilization, count every resource so capacity stays correct, and show a hidden
  resource only as occupied or used, never by name. This deliberately reveals
  that space is taken, not by what.

Every read path applies the same rule in its query:

* Fetching a hidden resource by ID behaves exactly like a missing one, so a URL
  or guessed ID never confirms that it exists.
* Lists, search, filters, sorting, pagination counts, Inbox and navigation
  badges, exports, and the Activity feed filter in the database before
  counting or limiting. A shared saved view shows each viewer only what they can
  read, as RFD 8 already requires.
* Live updates carry IDs, never another resource's data; each LiveView
  re-fetches through its own scope and drops what it can no longer see.
* Deciding a request requires reading its resource as well as the permission
  the change needs.
* RFD 12 sensitivity still applies on top: reading a resource never by itself
  reveals sensitive query results.

Losing read access takes effect like any other assignment change: connected
LiveViews refresh their scope and stop showing the resource, and an open page
for it returns to the list.

Machine scopes are not read-filtered; agents and reconcilers see what their
organization-scoped work needs.

== Enforcement

`Renga.Authorization` becomes the only place that answers authorization:

* `allowed?/3` takes a scope, a permission, and a target (or none for
  organization-wide actions), for UI hints;
* `authorize!/3` performs the same check inside a context transaction,
  re-reading the membership and its assignments under lock as today's checks
  re-read the membership row;
* `filter/3` adds the predicate for a permission to an Ecto query: for
  `inventory.read`, on every read path; for other permissions, on lists of
  actionable items and bulk actions. A bulk action refuses the whole batch if
  any target is not covered, rather than silently skipping it.

So that every filter is a single indexed join, Renga keeps one access row per
resource with the attributes conditions read: its kind, owner team and whether a
person set it, and current and confirmed site. The transaction that changes
placement or ownership updates that row too.

Every public context function that reads organization data declares the
permission it filters by. A test enumerates the contexts' public functions and
fails on any that declare none, and a scoped-member suite checks each list,
fetch, search, count, and export with a member who can see one site.

The scope carries the person's resolved assignments, loaded once per request or
LiveView mount. Assignment and team changes broadcast on the organization's
authorization topic so connected LiveViews refresh their scope, and expiring
assignments schedule a refresh like RFD 15's provider deadline. The transaction
check, not the cached scope, is authoritative.

Machine scopes are unchanged in shape: agents, the reconcilers, and triage rule
application run as fixed internal principals with fixed permission sets, which
cannot be assigned to people.

Some requirements stay outside the policy model as hard guards, checked after a
permission is granted: an active organization, organization enablement such as
allowed job capabilities, agent-local policy, step-up authentication (RFD 13),
second-operator approval (RFD 14), where the approver needs the same permission
over the target, and query sensitivity (RFD 12). Policy can only narrow who
reaches these guards; no assignment bypasses them.

[[administration]]
== Administration

* Members, custom roles, and assignments are managed in organization settings
  under sudo mode.
* An actor may create or assign a role only if the actor holds each of its
  permissions over at least the assignment's condition. Admins cannot grant
  `owner`, `roles.manage`, or `organization.manage`.
* `owner` is never a custom role or conditioned. An organization always keeps at
  least one active owner, which is its recovery path.
* Owners and admins may grant explicit permissions to themselves, as RFD 13
  allows for terminals; such assignments are audited like any other.
* Every change to a role definition, assignment, or team membership is
  recorded in Activity with its actor, before and after values, and reason. Expiry and source-driven removal are recorded too.

An access view answers both directions: on a member's page, what they can do
and through which assignments; on a resource page, who holds each permission
over it. A refused action names the missing permission, not just "forbidden".

== Relationship to other RFDs

* RFDs 12, 13, and 14 are published but not yet implemented. Their member
  query policy, terminal grants, and VM operator grants become assignments of
  explicit permissions. Their target sets (resources, a saved view, or an
  owning team) become conditions; a saved view is not a condition because its
  filters include observed data and its owner can edit it, so a grant from a
  view resolves to that view's current resource IDs. Those RFDs are amended once
  this one is published.
* RFD 15 claim mappings and RFD 16 group mappings produce source-bound
  assignments rather than setting a membership's role directly, so a mapping may
  grant a custom role or a conditioned one.
* RFD 8 teams keep owning resources; this RFD adds their members.

== Open questions

. *Admins and roles.* The proposal lets admins manage members and assignments
  but reserves custom role definitions for owners.
. *Subject-relative conditions.* `$subject_teams` is the main reason for team
  membership. The proposal includes it; the alternative is naming teams
  explicitly in every assignment.

== Future work

* Resource groups for boundaries that cut across sites and teams (RFD 18).
* Separation of duties beyond RFD 14's second-operator approval.
* Scoped API tokens for people, using the same assignments.
