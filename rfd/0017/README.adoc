= RFD 17 Attribute-based authorization

== Goal

Let an organization decide who may see and do what in which part of its
inventory.
Today every member holds one of four fixed roles (`owner`, `admin`, `member`,
`viewer`), and each context checks a hard-coded list of them. That cannot
express common arrangements: a network team that edits switches but not
power, facilities staff who place hardware at one site, or a contractor who may
only see and operate a few hosts for two weeks.

This RFD replaces the role lists with one model. A role is a named combination
of permissions. Attributes of the person decide which roles they hold, and
attributes of the resource decide where each role applies. RFDs 12, 13, and 14
already need scoped grants that can expire, and RFD 15 grants access from an
identity provider; all of them become instances of this model instead of
separate grant tables. A future directory synchronization design uses the same
source-bound assignment contract defined here.

Authorization stays where it is today: in context functions, inside the
transaction that performs the change. Hiding a control in the UI remains a hint,
never the boundary.

== Permissions

A permission names one kind of action, such as `inventory.edit` or
`topology.edit`. The catalogue is defined in code, not by organizations, and
every public context function that changes data declares and checks one or more
permissions or is an explicit system path.

The first release derives the catalogue from today's checks:

[cols="2,4"]
|===
|Permission |Allows

|`inventory.read`
|Seeing resources and everything that belongs to them (see <<reads>>)

|`inventory.reconcile`
|Reconciling observations into inventory, and accepting reconciliation results
 for topology and catalog

|`inventory.edit`
|Changing lifecycle state, setting and clearing field overrides, and creating
 or changing managed resource kinds

|`findings.act`
|Assigning, snoozing, and accepting exceptions on findings

|`requests.create`
|Proposing a change the person cannot make directly

|`requests.decide`
|Approving or rejecting proposals

|`catalog.author`
|Catalog types and drafts, modules, inventory items, and moves

|`catalog.expectations`
|Expected-component exceptions and automatic moves

|`dcim.edit`
|Sites, locations, racks, and placement

|`ipam.edit`
|VRFs, prefixes, addresses, plans, and source mappings

|`topology.edit`
|VLANs, cables, cable plans, and cable import contracts

|`jobs.request`
|Requesting, cancelling, and resolving `observe` jobs

|`views.share`
|Saving and changing shared views

|`teams.manage`
|Teams, team membership, and resource owners

|`triage_rules.manage`
|Triage rules

|`intake_keys.manage`
|Intake API keys

|`members.manage`
|Inviting and disabling members and changing their assignments

|`roles.manage`
|Defining custom roles

|`organization.manage`
|Organization settings, including job settings, appearance, security settings
and organization-local authorization settings. Identity providers remain
deployment configuration as RFD 15 requires

|`organization.export`
|Exporting the whole organization
|===

A setting controlled or locked by the deployment stays read-only even for
holders of `organization.manage`. No organization permission can change
deployment configuration or secrets.

Relationship checks such as "only the person who
made a request may withdraw it" and "a personal view belongs to its owner" stay
in context code; they are not permissions.

RFDs 12, 13, and 14 add `queries.run`, `queries.run_sensitive`,
`terminal.open`, `terminal.replay`, `vm.power`, and `vm.approve` when they are
implemented. Those are _explicit_ permissions: no built-in role includes them
and nobody holds them by default, including owners. Their grant and disclosure
contracts are:

[cols="2,3,3,2"]
|===
|Operation |Permission to act |Permission to read retained payload |Lifetime

|Inventory query
|`queries.run`
|`queries.run`
|Expiry optional

|Sensitive query, including ad hoc SQL
|`queries.run` and `queries.run_sensitive`
|`queries.run` and `queries.run_sensitive`
|Expiry optional

|Live terminal
|`terminal.open`
|`terminal.replay` to replay its recording
|`terminal.open` expires in at most 30 days

|VM power operation
|`vm.power`; a second operator also needs `vm.approve` and `vm.power`
|Job metadata requires `inventory.read`; payloads require `vm.power`
|`vm.power` and `vm.approve` must expire
|===

This deliberately replaces RFD 12's owner/admin and member-query role checks
with permissions while preserving its sensitivity split. It preserves RFD 13's
30-day terminal limit, replay restriction and replay audit, and RFD 14's
expiring grants and distinct second operator. A deployment or organization may
set a shorter maximum, never a longer one. Explicit permissions are kept in
roles that contain no non-explicit permissions, so a routine role never quietly
acquires operational access.

== Roles

A role is a named set of permissions within an organization.

The four built-in roles keep today's behavior exactly and cannot be edited:

[cols="3,1,1,1,1"]
|===
|Permission |`owner` |`admin` |`member` |`viewer`

|`inventory.read` |yes |yes |yes |yes
|`inventory.reconcile`, `findings.act`, `catalog.author` |yes |yes |yes |
|`requests.create` | | |yes |
|`inventory.edit`, `requests.decide`, `catalog.expectations`, `dcim.edit`,
 `ipam.edit`, `topology.edit`, `jobs.request`, `views.share`, `teams.manage`,
 `triage_rules.manage`, `intake_keys.manage` |yes |yes | |
|`members.manage`, `roles.manage` |yes |yes | |
|`organization.manage`, `organization.export` |yes | | |
|===

`members.manage` and `roles.manage` are new; Renga has no member management UI
today. Permission to administer a grant is separate from permission to perform
the granted action, so an owner or admin can still assign the built-in `member`
role without gaining `requests.create`. The grant ceilings and explicit
permission bootstrap rules are in <<administration>>.

Owners and admins can define custom roles from any non-explicit permissions,
for example a `network engineer` role with `inventory.reconcile`,
`findings.act`, `ipam.edit`, and `topology.edit`. Explicit permissions live in
their own roles as described above.

== Assignments

An assignment gives one role to a subject, optionally limited by a condition
and an expiry:

* _role_: a built-in or custom role;
* _subject_: one member, a team, a mapped claim value (RFD 15), or a directory
  group once directory synchronization is designed;
* _condition_: which resources it covers (below); none means the whole
  organization;
* _valid until_: optional, and required for explicit permissions when the
  operation table above requires it;
* _source_: `manual`, a claim mapping, or a future directory connection. An
  external assignment has a deadline issued only by its own source. It grants
  nothing after that deadline, and unlinking or removing its identity, mapping,
  or connection invalidates it immediately;
* who created it, when, and why.

Each membership keeps one base role, as today, which acts as an assignment
without a condition. A membership may instead have no base role, so that it
holds only what its other assignments grant (see <<reads>>). Every further assignment adds access; the result is the
union of everything a person holds. There are no deny rules, so the question
"why can this person do this" always has a list of assignments as its answer.
A manual assignment is never changed or removed by a claim mapping or directory
connection; an externally sourced one is removed when its source no longer
grants it.

Teams gain members. A team is already the owner of resources (RFD 8); adding
people to it lets one assignment say "people on a team may operate what their
team owns". Team membership is managed with `teams.manage`; a future directory
connection may manage only the membership of teams explicitly mapped to it.

== Conditions

A condition is a set of clauses over resource attributes. A resource matches
when it satisfies every clause, and a clause is satisfied by any of its values:

[source,json]
----
{
  "site": ["<site id>"],
  "kind": ["server", "switch"],
  "owner_team": ["$subject_teams"]
}
----

This reads as "servers and switches at this site that are owned by one of the
person's teams", and the UI renders it that way.

The first release supports these attributes:

* `resource`: explicit resource IDs;
* `kind`: resource kinds;
* `owner_team`: team IDs, or `$subject_teams` for the teams the person belongs
  to;
* `site`: site IDs, matched through the resource's current placement, or the
  resource itself for sites, locations, and racks.

The API rejects unknown condition attributes, operators, resource kinds, and
IDs from another organization. It never accepts an inert policy that only
appears to grant access.

Conditions are deliberately a small structured language rather than a general
policy language such as Cedar or Rego. Lists, search, and bulk actions must find
what a person may act on in the database, so every condition compiles to an SQL
predicate over indexed columns. A richer language would either leak into
per-row checks in Elixir or need its own partial evaluator.

Every capability declares how the server derives its complete authorization
target set. A permission is checked against every resource in that set; a
caller-supplied association or presentation `resource_id` never selects or
narrows the boundary. Placement, cables, and other actions that involve several
resources must be covered for each of them. An action whose target is
organization-wide, such as defining a catalog type, a VRF, a site, a team, or a
triage rule, requires the permission from an assignment without a condition.
Source- or agent-wide actions, unmatched observations, and reconciliation that
may create a resource also require an unconditioned permission unless the
capability can derive every affected resource before it changes data. Resource
creation authorizes its server-derived parent or container first and the
complete resulting target set before commit.

Conditions never read agent-reported data such as resource labels. An
organization configures its agents but does not fully control them, and anyone
holding its intake key can report any label. Boundaries that cut across sites
and teams, such as environment, customer, or compliance scope, are left to
RFD 18's resource groups.

=== Trusted attributes

Some attributes can be derived from observations: triage rules set owner teams
from reported data, and placement may come from observed evidence. Inventory
workflow permissions may match those derived values, but derived values never
create a confidentiality boundary. `inventory.read`, `jobs.request`, and the
explicit permissions only match trusted values: explicit resource IDs,
manually set or approved owner teams, and confirmed placement. A condition on
kind uses the canonical resource kind; it cannot turn observed ownership or
placement into a trusted match. This preserves RFD 13's rule that grants
authorize against trusted bindings and prevents a collector or intake-key
holder from moving existing hidden data into a reader's scope.

=== Changing attributes

Changing an attribute that a condition reads (owner team or placement) requires
the actor to cover the resource both before and after the change. Someone who
may edit servers at one site cannot move a server to another site they do not
cover, and cannot move one into their own scope. Approving a request applies the
same rule to the approver. Because team membership, ownership, and placement can
change authorization without changing an assignment, each such change is also
validated as a grant transition under <<administration>> before it commits.

[[reads]]
== Reads

`inventory.read` is a permission like any other, and conditions limit it. A
person sees a resource only when one of their assignments grants
`inventory.read` with a condition the resource matches. Every built-in role
includes it, so a member whose base role is built-in still reads the whole
organization, as today. A contractor can instead be a member with no base role
and a `viewer` assignment for hosts at one site, and sees nothing else.

What a resource's visibility covers:

* Everything that belongs to one resource follows it: its fields, observations
  and evidence, interfaces, components and modules, assigned addresses,
  placement, findings, requests, jobs, and Activity entries.
* Sites, locations, and racks are resources and follow the same rule, except
  that a person also sees the containers of every resource they can see, so its
  placement can be shown.
* Organization-wide definitions (catalog types, VRFs, prefixes, VLAN groups and
  VLANs, and teams) are shared vocabulary, visible to anyone who holds
  `inventory.read` anywhere.
* Records that join several resources, such as cables, neighbor adjacency, and
  power paths, show only the endpoints the person can see. A hidden endpoint
  appears as not visible to them, without its name or ID.
* Aggregates over a shared container, such as rack occupancy and prefix
  utilization, count every resource so capacity stays correct, and show a hidden
  resource only as occupied or used, never by name. This deliberately reveals
  that space is taken, not by what.

Every read path applies the same rule in its query:

* Fetching a hidden resource by ID behaves exactly like a missing one, so a URL
  or guessed ID never confirms that it exists.
* Lists, search, filters, sorting, pagination counts, Inbox and navigation
  badges, exports, and the Activity feed filter in the database before
  counting or limiting. A shared saved view shows each viewer only what they can
  read, as RFD 8 already requires.
* Live updates carry IDs, never another resource's data; each LiveView
  re-fetches through its own scope and drops what it can no longer see.
* Deciding a request requires reading its resource as well as the permission
  the change needs.
* RFD 12 sensitivity still applies on top: reading a resource never by itself
  reveals sensitive query results.

Losing read access takes effect like any other assignment change: connected
LiveViews refresh their scope and stop showing the resource, and an open page
for it returns to the list.

Machine scopes are not read-filtered; agents and reconcilers see what their
organization-scoped work needs.

== Enforcement

`Renga.Authorization` becomes the only place that answers authorization:

* `allowed?/3` takes a scope, a permission, and a target (or none for
  organization-wide actions), for UI hints;
* `authorize!/3` performs the same check inside a context transaction,
  re-reading the membership and every authorization dependency under the common
  authorization lock;
* `filter/3` adds the predicate for a permission to an Ecto query: for
  `inventory.read`, on every read path; for other permissions, on lists of
  actionable items and bulk actions. A bulk action refuses the whole batch if
  any target is not covered, rather than silently skipping it.

Renga keeps one access row per resource with the attributes conditions read:
its kind, owner team and whether a person set it, and current and confirmed
site. The transaction that changes placement or ownership updates that row too.
Inherited containers, mixed-resource records and redacted aggregates still use
query-specific joins or projections; the access row does not make an opaque
payload safe to disclose.

Every public context function that reads organization data declares the
permission it filters by. A test enumerates the contexts' public functions and
fails on any that declare none, and a scoped-member suite checks each list,
fetch, search, count, and export with a member who can see one site.

The scope carries the person's resolved assignments and the organization's
authorization version, loaded once per request or LiveView mount. Every change
to a role definition, membership status or base role, assignment, team
membership, or external source validity increments that version and broadcasts
on the organization's authorization topic. Connected LiveViews refresh before
protected data delivery or events when the version changes. They also check the
earliest assignment or provider deadline before delivery; a timer is only a
wake-up mechanism, never enforcement. The transaction check, current version,
and current deadlines, not the cached scope, are authoritative.

Every authorization-changing transaction locks the organization's
authorization-version row before its dependencies. Protected mutations take
the same lock before authorization. Role, membership, assignment, team and
source changes therefore serialize with decisions they can affect. A resource
access-row change locks that row and evaluates the old and new target under the
same transaction, so neither a stale policy nor stale placement can authorize a
commit.

Machine scopes are unchanged in shape: agents, the reconcilers, and triage rule
application run as fixed internal principals with fixed permission sets, which
cannot be assigned to people.

Some requirements stay outside the policy model as hard guards, checked after a
permission is granted: an active organization, deployment entitlements and
locks, organization enablement such as allowed job capabilities, agent-local
policy, step-up authentication (RFD 13), second-operator approval (RFD 14), and
query result classification (RFD 12). The operation table defines the additional
permissions for approval and sensitive disclosure. Policy can only narrow who
reaches these guards; no assignment bypasses them.

[[administration]]
== Administration

* Members, custom roles, and assignments are managed in organization settings.
  Like every other change, they rely on the actor's permissions and the
  Activity record rather than asking the actor to sign in again.
* Grant administration has a code-defined ceiling separate from action
  permissions. A built-in owner may grant any built-in role and any
  non-explicit permission. A built-in admin may grant `admin`, `member`, or
  `viewer` and any non-explicit permission except `organization.manage` and
  `organization.export`; this includes `requests.create`, although the admin
  cannot create requests. Owners and admins may grant the explicit permissions
  whose source RFD authorizes them to issue, including to themselves. This is
  the bootstrap path for permissions nobody initially holds.
* `members.manage` and `roles.manage` are reserved to the built-in owner and
  admin roles; custom roles cannot carry them. Creating, changing, or deleting
  a role and assigning it requires both the matching management permission and
  every permission in the role to be inside the actor's grant ceiling, before
  and after the change. Admins cannot grant `owner` or either owner-only
  organization permission.
* Changing a role changes access for everyone who holds it, so the role page
  shows how many people and assignments it affects before a change is saved.
* A conditioned grant must be contained by the actor's grant-administration
  scope for every recipient. `$subject_teams` and other recipient-relative or
  dynamically expanding conditions require unconditioned grant authority for
  every permission in their role; textual equality and the resources matching
  at creation time are not containment proofs.
* Team membership, externally synchronized membership, role changes, and
  trusted ownership or placement changes are grant transitions. Before commit,
  Renga calculates the effective permissions each recipient gains and requires
  the actor's grant ceiling to cover each permission and newly covered resource.
  A holder of `teams.manage` without the corresponding grant authority may edit
  team metadata but cannot add an authorization-bearing member or move a
  resource into a grant's scope.
* `owner` is never a custom role or conditioned. An organization always keeps at
  least one active membership whose built-in `owner` base role is manual,
  direct, and non-expiring. That recovery owner cannot be disabled or removed
  until another exists. Additional owner assignments may be expiring or
  externally sourced, but an expired or revoked source is never retained to
  satisfy recovery.
* Every change to a role definition, assignment, or team membership is
  recorded in Activity with its actor, before and after values, and reason.
  Expiry and source-driven removal are recorded too.

An access view answers both directions: on a member's page, what they can do
and through which assignments; on a resource page, who holds each permission
over it. A refused action names the missing permission, not just "forbidden".

== Relationship to other RFDs

* RFDs 12, 13, and 14 are published but not yet implemented. Their member
  query policy, terminal grants, and VM operator grants become assignments of
  explicit permissions. Their target sets (resources, a saved view, or an
  owning team) become conditions; a saved view is not a condition because its
  filters include observed data and its owner can edit it, so a grant from a
  view resolves to that view's current resource IDs. Those RFDs are amended once
  this one is published.
* RFD 15 claim mappings and RFD 16 group mappings produce source-bound
  assignments rather than setting a membership's role directly, so a mapping may
  grant a custom role or a conditioned one.
* RFD 8 teams keep owning resources; this RFD adds their members.
